Your first 15 minutes with Muse

Do this once, in order, the first time you sign in. One finished, checked result teaches more than an hour of feature tours.

  1. Minutes 0–2 · Confirm you are really inOpen Settings. Check your account email, time zone, plan and token balance. If you have a code, redeem it now in Settings > Redeem token — GZTOI6 if you want to use the Muse Playbooks code — and confirm the balance moved.
  2. Minutes 2–4 · Give Muse one rule to keepSay one standing rule out loud in chat, for example your pay floor, your work hours, or “drafts wait for my approval, never send on your own.” Ask Muse to repeat back exactly what it saved, in one line. If the repeat-back is wrong, fix it now.
  3. Minutes 4–6 · Pick a job with a clear doneChoose something small you already need: rank a short list, summarize a note you wrote, draft a reply, or validate one idea. Write what “done” looks like in one sentence before you prompt.
  4. Minutes 6–11 · Run it with context and a QC step in the same promptPaste your numbers and source material, state the output format, and end with: “Check every name, date and number against the source I gave you, and flag anything you had to guess.”
  5. Minutes 11–13 · Check the result yourselfRead it against the source. Open any file it made — do not trust the chat summary alone. Fix one thing and note what was wrong; that note is your first QC habit.
  6. Minutes 13–15 · Log it and save what workedStart a one-line tracker entry: date, job, result, keep / fix / kill. If the prompt worked, save it. You now have access, one rule saved, one checked result, and a tracker started — that is a real first session.

Troubleshooting

Stuck? Work through these cases

Most new-user blocks are one of these eight. Find your case, do the fix in order, and only move on when the check passes.

1. “I cannot sign up at all”

Likely cause: your country is Not yet, or you are on a waitlist. Fix: check the Availability Checker, join the official waitlist once with real details, and re-check after announcements. Do not use a VPN or borrowed address. Check: you can state clearly whether you are Live, waitlisted, or Not yet.

2. “My code will not redeem”

Likely cause: typo, past the 48-hour window, or already redeemed on this account. Fix: retype GZTOI6 exactly in Settings > Redeem token, check the window, check your balance history, then use official support. Never buy a replacement code from a stranger.

3. “Muse gave me a confident wrong answer”

Fix: ask for sources, open the source yourself, and re-run with “verify against this source and flag guesses.” For prices, dates and job details, the source wins every time.

4. “The output is generic and useless”

Likely cause: no context, no numbers, no format. Fix: add your floor or budget, your source material, an example of good, and a done definition. Vague in, vague out is not a Muse failure.

5. “A feature I read about is missing”

Likely cause: announced, not live for your plan or region yet. Fix: check your own account, keep the manual way of doing that job, and re-check after announcements. Do not rebuild a workflow around it.

6. “A site blocked Muse / showed a bot wall”

Fix: stop at the first confirmed block. Confirm once in a normal browser, log it once with the date, and reroute: manual use, official alerts or API, or another platform. No bypass tools, solvers, or retry loops.

7. “My dashboard or tracker is stale”

Likely cause: work was done in chat and never logged, or a cron stopped. Fix: reconcile today from mail and notes, stamp the new reconciled date, and set one weekly sweep cron that reports only gaps.

8. “I am burning tokens with nothing to show”

Fix: size the job first in the Token Estimator, cut the prompt to one job per run, cap reply length, and check your balance in Settings before any multi-step run. Small finished jobs beat one giant run.

What is Muse?

An AI agent that does work, not just chat

Muse is Meta's personal AI agent. Beyond answering questions, it can research, draft, build pages and documents, work through multi-step tasks, and use skills — reusable instructions for jobs you repeat. You stay in charge: you approve sends, payments and submissions.

What is a playbook?

A method with steps, a check, and a stop rule

A playbook on Muse Playbooks is not a vague guide. It has: the goal, the setup numbers, numbered steps, a QC check before you ship, and a kill rule that tells you when to quit a lane that isn't paying.

What is a skill?

Saved instructions Muse reuses

A skill is a playbook Muse itself follows. Instead of re-explaining your rules every time — your price floor, your QC list, your format — you save them once as a skill and Muse applies them on every run.

What is a dashboard?

One pinned page that shows where you stand

A dashboard is built from a tracker or ledger — applications, income, books, batches, jobs — and pinned so it is the first thing you open. It keeps no numbers of its own: every total traces to the source underneath it, and it shows when it was last updated.

What is a cron?

A job Muse runs on a schedule

A cron runs whether or not you open a chat: a weekly sweep, a deadline watch, a payment alert. It updates your tracker or dashboard and reports only what needs a decision. Crons draft, log and alert — they never send, pay or submit without your go.

What are connectors & memory?

What Muse can reach, and what it remembers

Connectors link accounts Muse can work with, like email, calendar and files — only connect what a real job needs, and test that the connection is live. Memory is standing context saved between sessions: your floors, formats and rules, so you stop re-typing them.

What most new users miss

Muse does not know your rules until you save them

A good answer once is not a system. Memory and skills are what make it repeat.

+

New users re-explain the same floor, format or preference in every chat and wonder why results drift. The fix is boring: say the rule once, ask Muse to confirm exactly what it saved in one line, and put repeat jobs into a skill. If a rule matters — a pay floor, a price, a format, a no-guessing rule — and it is not saved, assume the next run does not have it.

Muse can sound right and still be wrong

Check the source, and check the finished file — not the chat reply.

+

Prices, dates, job details and program terms go stale or get copied wrong. For anything you will act on, verify at the source: the employer's own page, the official listing, the actual payout record. And when Muse builds a document or file, open the exported file and read it — generated files can quietly truncate text or misalign numbers while reporting the right page count. Every playbook on this site has a QC step for this reason. Do not skip it to save five minutes.

Approvals are yours, and they are per action

Drafts stage and wait. Nothing should send, pay or submit on autopilot.

+

Set Muse up so replies, proposals, applications and purchases are drafted and staged for your approval, not sent. Approving a draft once does not approve the next one. Never hand over passwords, payment card numbers, or one-time codes for accounts you are not actively signing into at Muse's request, and keep other people's private details and client data out of anything public.

Job Hunt — Any Work Type

Works for remote, hybrid and onsite roles. The method is the same: define the job you can accept before you look, then protect yourself with QC.

  1. Set your floor firstMinimum pay, work type, location or time zone, schedule, and benefits you need. Write it down before searching.
  2. Search to your floor, not to a vibeRank roles against your actual experience and proof — a portfolio and measurable results beat claims, degree or not.
  3. Verify on the employer's own pageListings get copied and go stale. Confirm title, pay range, work type and requirements at the source.
  4. Tailor honestlyMatch your real work to the posting's language. Reframe, never invent.
  5. Compare total payBase + employer retirement match + health costs + PTO + schedule. A higher headline salary can pay less overall.
  6. QC before submit — every timeName, contact, work history, education, salary, and the exact job title and company. Never guess a screening answer; if you don't know, check or leave it for review.
  7. Track everythingCompany, role, date, status, next step. Check for a prior application before applying again.
QC checkWould every line on this application survive the employer reading it back to you in an interview? If not, fix it before submit.

  6. Set skills, rate and availability. Add the skill tags buyers actually search for, set your rate or starting price at your floor, and select your real availability. A profile that promises everything converts worse than one that promises one thing well.
  7. Add the portfolio. Upload 3–5 samples in the platform's portfolio section (see the Proof Portfolio guide below). Each gets a descriptive title, two lines on what it was and for whom, and the result where you have one. Remove or redact client-confidential details; keep the professional formatting — it is part of the proof.
  8. Finish the business settings. Add your payment method, complete any tax or payout forms the platform requires, and set notifications so new invitations and client messages reach you fast. Slow replies lose jobs that good profiles win.
  9. Build saved searches. Filter job listings by your service, your minimum budget, and your maximum scope. Prefer clients with a work history and verified payment where the platform shows it. Save the search and check it on a schedule instead of scrolling the open feed.
  10. Evaluate before you apply. Score each job: skill match, budget vs your floor, scope clarity, client signals. Vague scope at a low budget is the most expensive job on any platform — skip it.
  11. Apply through the platform's own flow. Send a tailored proposal (the Advanced Proposal prompt on this page writes it): their problem, your closest proof, your plan, your price and scope, one next step. Answer every screening question honestly. Use the standard application cost where the platform charges credits — skip paid boosts until a lane is already converting.
  12. Log it and work the thread. Record platform, client, job, date and status in your tracker. If the client replies, keep the conversation in that thread and on-platform, follow up once if it goes quiet, and never start work without the platform's contract or escrow step in place where it offers one.
  13. Review weekly. Applications sent, reply rate, interviews, wins. Fix the weakest number first — usually the profile headline or the first two lines of the proposal — and cut job categories that never convert.

Proof Portfolio — Build Once, Reuse Everywhere

One master portfolio you can drop into any platform profile, proposal or page without rebuilding it.

+

Guide

  1. Pick your three strongest real pieces. Finished, paid or shipped work beats practice pieces. If you are starting out, build three samples in your lane's real format, to a real standard.
  2. Sanitize without gutting. Remove client names, personal data and anything confidential. Keep the layout, headings, tables and formatting exactly as delivered — on documentation and design work, the formatting is itself the sample.
  3. Write a case card for each piece. Five lines: the problem, who it was for (generalized), what you delivered, the format, and the result — a number wherever an honest one exists.
  4. Export in platform-friendly forms. Keep each piece as a clean PDF and, where it helps, a public view-only link. Name files clearly (Service-Type-Sample.pdf) so they still make sense detached from your profile.
  5. Keep a text version of every case card. One document with all five-line summaries, ready to paste into profile fields, proposal forms and "about" sections that do not accept uploads.
  6. Store one master folder. Portfolio pieces, case cards, your headline and overview text, and your rate/scope basics live in one place. Every platform profile is filled from this folder — never typed fresh from memory.
  7. Refresh after every paid job. Swap the weakest sample out, update the result lines, and note which sample gets mentioned by clients. Within a few jobs, your portfolio stops being a guess and starts being evidence of what sells.

Ranks roles against your resume and floor, drafts applications under a no-guessing rule, preps interviews from your real work.

+
Inputs
Your resume, pay floor, work type (remote / hybrid / onsite), location or time zone, schedule, must-have benefits, and your standing screening answers (work authorization, sponsorship, clearance).
Output
A ranked shortlist with match reasons, a tailored draft per role, and interview prep tied to your actual experience.
Steps it runs
  1. Search to your floor — roles below it are dropped before you ever see them.
  2. Rank each role against your resume: real matches first, stretch roles labeled as stretch.
  3. Verify title, pay and requirements on the employer’s own page — aggregator copies go stale.
  4. Check your mail / tracker for a prior application to the same company and role.
  5. Draft the application honestly: reframe real work in the posting’s language, invent nothing.
  6. Prep interview answers from the same proof the application used.
QC checklist
  • Name, contact, work history, education and salary match your records exactly.
  • Job title and company match the posting being answered.
  • Every screening answer comes from your standing answers — anything unknown is flagged, never guessed.
  • Certifications described accurately (in-progress is stated as in-progress).
Failure modes

Stale listings copied across boards; portals with no guest / Google / LinkedIn sign-in that burn an hour for one application; headline salary that loses once match, health costs and PTO are counted. The skill’s rule: verify at source, compare total pay, skip broken funnels unless the match and pay are exceptional.

The missing resources — in full

The stuff most hustle content skips — and the stuff that actually decides whether a lane survives.

The money system

Separate account, tax set-aside, expenses tracked, budget on your lowest month.

+

Side income is taxed on top of a paycheck, so it lands in your highest bracket. Working system: keep side income in a separate account, move roughly 30% of profit aside for taxes as it arrives, track every expense and business mile when it happens (not in April), and budget the lane on your lowest month, not your best. What breaks people is not the tax rate — it is spending gross and meeting net later.

The full system: ledger, set-aside rule, monthly close →

No-degree proof path

A portfolio of real work beats claims. One credible cert for your lane, not ten.

+

Employers and clients pay for proof: samples of real work (redacted where needed), measurable results, and references. Build three strong samples in your lane’s real format, keep their original professional formatting — the formatting itself is evidence — then add one credible certification that the lane actually respects. Ten half-finished certs signal dabbling; proof plus one cert signals hireable.

Freelance pricing & negotiation

Quote before work, deposit up front, scope in writing, negotiate the whole deal.

+

Never start unquoted. A workable pattern: flat quote in writing, a modest deposit before work begins, parts or extras agreed before you buy anything, and any sourcing fee agreed in advance — never a surprise. When negotiating a salary or rate, negotiate total pay: base, match, health costs, PTO, timeline and revisions. The headline number is the easiest one to move and often the least valuable.

First clients & reviews

Your first 10 jobs buy ratings and proof. Ask once, save everything.

+

Early jobs are marketing spend disguised as work: price fairly, deliver slightly more than promised, and finish cleanly. Then ask once for a review — one message, both review links — and never chase a second ask, especially with someone who already reviewed you. Save every result, photo (with permission, no personal details) and kind word as a case study; that file is what your next ten clients are actually buying.

Day-job math

Match timing, health costs, open enrollment, PTO — quitting at the wrong week is expensive.

+

Before changing jobs, run the calendar: employer retirement match vesting and eligibility dates, whether you must be employed on the last day of a plan year to receive a match, open enrollment windows, health-plan costs on both sides, tobacco or wellness surcharges and how their refund rules actually trigger, and PTO payout rules. A raise that lands one week too early or too late can quietly cost more than it gains. Compare total pay across a full year, not the first paycheck.

Time & automation system

Cap a new lane at 5–10 hours a week. Automate anything you do three times.

+

One lane until it pays. Cap it at 5–10 hours a week to start so it survives a bad work week. Anything you do three times gets a template, a checklist, or a skill — that is exactly what Muse skills are for. Keep a simple weekly log: shipped, failed, one number, one lesson. If a tool, site or portal is confirmed blocked, log it once and reroute; do not pay rent to a dead route with daily retries.

Scam & hype filter

No real numbers, no fees shown, no time to first dollar? Skip it.

+

Three questions end most hype: What are the real numbers? (not testimonials — medians). What are all the fees? (platform, payment, materials, your hours). When is the first dollar, in weeks? If a pitch cannot answer all three, it is selling the pitch. And remember: “low competition” with no buyers is not an opportunity, and rewards paid in tokens or points are not cash until they convert.

+

+

The fix is boring and total: export the real file, open it, and read it — especially instruction pages, disclaimers and anything auto-wrapped. AutomationJudgment

Stop at a confirmed block

+

Automation fails in two ways: stopping too early, and grinding forever against a wall — a blocked site, a closed portal, a route that no longer exists. When a block is confirmed (not guessed), the productive move is to stop, log it, and reroute your hours. Retrying a dead route daily isn't persistence; it's a subscription to frustration. Note the block once, clearly, and move on.

AutomationAdvanced

When a platform blocks you: Cloudflare, bot walls, and the stop rule

+

What it looks like. A "Verify you are human" or "Checking your browser" screen that never clears. A CAPTCHA that loops no matter how many times you solve it. 403 or 429 errors on pages that open fine for everyone else. Or the quiet version: you can sign in, but messages, searches or applications silently fail or never update. If you rely on automated access to a freelance or job platform, this is the failure you will eventually meet.

Why it happens. Most large platforms sit behind bot management — Cloudflare is the common one — that scores every visit: IP reputation (shared, VPN and data-center ranges score badly), browser fingerprint, request speed and pattern, and signals that a browser is automated rather than hand-driven. Advanced users trip it precisely because their setup looks like bot traffic: scripted page loads, rapid repeated checks, unusual browser fingerprints. Many platforms also prohibit automated access in their terms, so the wall is policy, not just a glitch.

What to do. Stop at the first confirmed block. Confirm it once, yourself, in a normal browser the way a regular user would — if the platform works there, the wall is aimed at the automated route, not at you. If it blocks normal human use too, use the platform's official support channel. Then stop. Do not try to bypass it: no CAPTCHA-solving services, no fingerprint spoofing, no rotating proxies, no retry loops. A bypass attempt can escalate a soft block into an account flag or a ban — and losing the account costs far more than losing the route. Hammering a wall also burns the one resource you cannot refill: your hours.

Park the route, use a lane that is open. Log the block once, with the date, and mark the route parked in your tracker so you stop paying it daily attention. Then move the same work to a channel that is open: do it manually in the platform's own website or app, turn on the platform's official email alerts and saved-search notifications instead of scraping for updates, use an official API if the platform offers one, or take the lane to a different platform. Keep your proposals, portfolio and tracker in your own files, so a parked route never strands the business that ran on it.

What it means for advanced users. Any workflow built on automated access to someone else's platform is rented, not owned — it can end on any Tuesday, without notice, and arguing with the wall will not bring it back. Build accordingly: every automated step gets a manual fallback you have actually tested, your records live outside the platform, and no single route carries a whole income lane. The durable edge was never the automation itself. It is the process, the proof and the QC — and those still work, by hand, on the lanes that are open.

MarketsReality check

Low competition can mean low demand — and tokens aren't cash

+

New platforms always look like open fields: few guides, few creators, little competition. Sometimes that's an opportunity. Often it's a signal that the audience is still small. The same caution applies to rewards: referral programs that pay in tokens, credits or points can be genuinely useful, but they aren't income until they convert to money. Evaluate every “opportunity” in dollars and weeks, not in vibes.

TRACK

Track taxes & expenses: the ledger

Every payment and expense logged the day it happens, with the set-aside moved before the money gets spent.

+
Inputs
Every payment (date, source, gross, platform fees, net) and every expense (date, category, amount, what it was for), plus business mileage.
Output
One reconciled ledger per business, a set-aside balance that matches it, and a monthly close.
Steps
  1. Log the payment the day it lands. Date, lane or source, gross, fees taken, net received. Gross is not money you can spend.
  2. Move the set-aside immediately. The Muse Playbooks working rule is 30% of profit into a separate tax holding account, before the rest is touched.
  3. Log expenses the same day. Software, tools, supplies, content costs, and mileage with date, purpose and miles.
  4. Keep categories boring and fixed. Income, fees, software, tools, supplies, mileage, other. New categories hide spending; fixed ones expose it.
  5. Keep separate businesses separate. A line with its own tax reporting, like publishing, gets its own ledger, not a shared pile.
  6. Close the month. Total income, total expenses, profit, set-aside owed vs set-aside held. Fix gaps now, not at filing time.
QC checklist
  • Every payment in the ledger traces to a real deposit or payout record.
  • Set-aside held matches set-aside owed on logged profit.
  • Every expense has a category and a business purpose; no personal spending mixed in.
  • Mileage entries carry date, purpose and miles, logged when driven.
Failure modes

Rebuilding a year of records from memory in April. Spending gross and meeting net at filing time. Mixing two businesses, or business and personal, in one undifferentiated list.

Log this payment in my tax ledger: [source, gross, fees, date]. Move the 30% set-aside on the profit, then show me set-aside owed vs held and anything uncategorized this month.

What breaks this system

The failure modes, and the fix for each

Stale panels, hand-typed totals, ghost crons, and dashboards nobody opens.

+
  • Stale dashboard trusted as current: no reconciled date, or a cron that quietly stopped. Fix: date at the top, and a cron that reports its own failure once.
  • Hand-typed totals: the dashboard drifts from the tracker within weeks. Fix: totals computed from the source only.
  • Five dashboards, none updated: one per idea instead of one per live lane. Fix: one pinned dashboard per live lane, main view only.
  • Cron guessing to look complete: ambiguous items silently categorized. Fix: flag-and-wait is a written guardrail in every cron prompt.
  • Ghost crons: scheduled jobs still running for killed lanes, reporting on nothing. Fix: killing a lane deletes its cron and its panel in the same pass.
  • Tracking in chat: the work exists only in conversation history. Fix: if it matters, it gets a row the same day.
